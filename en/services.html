Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)

Persistently elevated blood pressure that strains the heart and blood vessels.

Read more

Hypertension usually causes no symptoms but raises the risk of heart attack, stroke, heart failure, and kidney disease. It is diagnosed with repeated measurements and often confirmed with home or ambulatory monitoring.

Most people can reach their goal with a combination of diet, exercise, and medication.

Common symptoms

  • Usually none
  • Headache or blurred vision when very high
Call 911 if you haveBlood pressure above 180/120 with chest pain, shortness of breath, severe headache, confusion, or weakness

Related services & tests

Hyperlipidemia (High Cholesterol)

Elevated blood lipids that contribute to plaque buildup in the arteries.

Read more

Cholesterol goals depend on your overall cardiovascular risk. Treatment is tailored to you and may include diet, exercise, statins, and newer lipid-lowering therapies when needed.

Common symptoms

  • Usually none
Call 911 if you haveNone specific; see the warning signs under angina and myocardial infarction

Related services & tests

Cardiovascular Prevention · Cardiology Consultation

Angina Pectoris

Chest discomfort caused by reduced blood flow to the heart muscle, usually from narrowed coronary arteries.

Read more

Angina is a symptom of coronary artery disease. It typically appears with exertion or stress and eases with rest. Evaluation may include an EKG, stress testing, echocardiography, or coronary CT angiography.

Treatment combines lifestyle changes and medications, and in some cases procedures such as stenting or bypass surgery performed at a hospital.

Common symptoms

  • Chest pressure, tightness, or heaviness with activity
  • Discomfort in the arm, jaw, neck, or back
  • Shortness of breath
  • Fatigue
Call 911 if you haveChest pain at rest lasting more than a few minutes
Chest pain with sweating, nausea, or shortness of breath
Chest pain not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin

Myocardial Infarction (Heart Attack)

Damage to the heart muscle when a coronary artery becomes suddenly blocked.

Read more

A heart attack is a medical emergency treated in the hospital. After a heart attack, ongoing cardiology care is essential: medications to protect the heart, cardiac rehabilitation, risk-factor control, and monitoring of heart function with echocardiography or cardiac MRI.

We provide post-heart-attack follow-up care and secondary prevention.

Common symptoms

  • Chest pressure or pain that may spread to the arm, jaw, or back
  • Shortness of breath
  • Cold sweat, nausea, light-headedness
  • In women and older adults, symptoms may be subtle: fatigue, indigestion-like discomfort
Call 911 if you haveAny of the symptoms above — call 911 immediately. Do not drive yourself to the hospital.

Congestive Heart Failure

A condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs.

Read more

Heart failure does not mean the heart has stopped; it means the heart muscle is weakened or stiff. With guideline-directed medications, device therapy when appropriate, and careful self-management of diet, fluids, and activity, many people live full lives and stay out of the hospital.

Common symptoms

  • Shortness of breath with activity or when lying flat
  • Swelling of the legs or abdomen
  • Rapid weight gain from fluid
  • Fatigue
Call 911 if you haveSevere shortness of breath at rest
Coughing up pink, frothy sputum
Fainting

Cardiac Arrhythmias

Abnormal heart rhythms, including atrial fibrillation, that can cause palpitations, fainting, or stroke.

Read more

Arrhythmias range from harmless extra beats to serious rhythm disorders. Atrial fibrillation, the most common sustained arrhythmia, raises the risk of stroke. Evaluation may include an EKG, wearable rhythm monitors, echocardiography, and exercise testing.

Dr. Cho's research background is in cardiac arrhythmia. Management focuses on preventing stroke, controlling heart rate, and restoring normal rhythm with medication or, when appropriate, referral for catheter ablation or device therapy.

Common symptoms

  • Palpitations or fluttering
  • Dizziness or light-headedness
  • Shortness of breath
  • Fainting
  • Sometimes no symptoms
Call 911 if you haveFainting or near-fainting with palpitations
Sudden weakness or numbness on one side, trouble speaking, or facial droop (possible stroke)
Chest pain

Cardiology Consultation

A thorough evaluation of your heart health, history, and risk factors, with a personalized plan.

A new-patient consultation reviews your symptoms, medical and family history, medications, and any prior test results, followed by a focused physical examination and an electrocardiogram (EKG) when indicated.

You will leave with a clear explanation of the findings and next steps, which may include additional testing, lifestyle changes, or medication.

How to prepare

  • Bring a list of all medications and supplements.
  • Bring prior test results or hospital records if available.
  • Bring your insurance card and photo ID.

Echocardiogram

An ultrasound of the heart that shows how the chambers and valves are working.

A transthoracic echocardiogram uses sound waves to create moving images of your heart. It is painless, involves no radiation, and takes about 30–45 minutes.

Studies are interpreted by Dr. Cho, who is certified by the National Board of Echocardiography.

How to prepare

  • No special preparation is needed.
  • Wear a two-piece outfit for comfort.

Exercise Stress Test

A treadmill test that shows how your heart performs under exertion.

Exercise stress testing helps evaluate chest pain, shortness of breath, exercise capacity, and exercise-related rhythm problems. Your EKG, heart rate, and blood pressure are monitored continuously by a clinician throughout the test.

How to prepare

  • Do not eat for 3 hours before the test.
  • Avoid caffeine for 24 hours unless told otherwise.
  • Ask us whether to take your usual medications the morning of the test.
  • Wear comfortable clothing and walking shoes.

Vascular Ultrasound

Ultrasound imaging of the carotid arteries and blood vessels of the arms and legs.

Vascular ultrasound evaluates blood flow and plaque in the carotid arteries and peripheral vessels. It is painless and involves no radiation. It is commonly used to assess stroke risk and peripheral artery disease.

CT Coronary Angiogram

A non-invasive CT scan that visualizes the coronary arteries and detects narrowing or plaque.

Coronary CT angiography uses contrast dye and a CT scanner to produce detailed images of the heart's arteries. It is often used to evaluate chest pain or to assess coronary risk when other tests are inconclusive.

Cardiac MRI

Detailed magnetic resonance imaging of the heart's structure, function, and scar.

Cardiac MRI provides highly detailed images without radiation and is useful for evaluating cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, prior heart attack, and congenital heart disease.

Cardiovascular Prevention

Risk assessment and treatment to prevent heart attack and stroke.

We evaluate cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar, family history, and other risk factors, and may use tools such as coronary calcium scoring to personalize your prevention plan.
